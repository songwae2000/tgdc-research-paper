\section*{Question}

Labs generate enterprise data from procedurally built companies: an org chart, a
taxonomy of work, and hand-written rules for how that work behaves. If domain
reasoning can supply the behaviour, a generator can be built for a client
without their records.

This paper tests whether it can, for one concrete property of operational work:
how long a task takes.

\section*{Hypothesis}

\textbf{H.} Domain priors reasoned from the nature of the work capture a substantial
share of what real operational records provide for predicting task duration.

Recorded before evaluation: a prior written without sight of the target data
should reach an AUC of 0.60 to 0.72. Below 0.55 would mean domain reasoning
supplies essentially nothing.

\textbf{Why it matters.} If H holds, a taxonomy is enough to build a generator. If it
fails, the behaviour has to come from the client's own data.

\section*{Method}

\textbf{Data} (Table 1). Public 311 service request feeds from four cities. These are
real operational records, written by the organisations that do the work.
Structurally they are a field service queue: work type, owning department,
intake channel, open and close timestamps. Two complete calendar weeks from
each, the weeks of 4 May and 8 June 2026.

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{lrrrr}
\toprule
city & train & test & median resolution & categories \\
\midrule
New York & 72,206 & 78,296 & 5.4h & 164 \\
Chicago & 17,291 & 38,671 & 118.0h & 92 \\
Austin & 5,968 & 6,029 & 23.8h & 117 \\
San Francisco & 17,723 & 17,007 & 15.1h & 37 \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{the four feeds, two complete weeks from each.}
\end{table*}

Tickets still open at the pull date are kept and treated as slow, since all are
months past any threshold used. Chicago closes most of its feed within a second
of creation, so those informational records are excluded.

\textbf{Task.} At ticket opening, predict whether it will take longer than the median
to resolve. Recovered skill is the share of the achievable advantage over chance
that an arm reaches: \texttt{(arm - 0.5) / (ceiling - 0.5)}.

\textbf{The ceiling is the prior's own information.} A prior reasons over the service
category and the owning department. Fitting the conditional rate directly from
those same two columns is a strong reference for what those columns support.
Comparing against it separates a weak rule from weak features. It is one
unregularised estimator rather than a proved upper bound, and a rule can exceed
it.

\textbf{Two resampling units.} A prior is a function of the category, so it takes one
value for every ticket in a category. Resampling tickets treats 17,007 San
Francisco rows as independent when the design has 37 independent units.
Intervals are reported both ways, and the category interval is the one any claim
about unseen work has to survive [1].

\textbf{Blind authorship.} Rules were written from each city's service type and
department vocabulary alone. The catalogue was pulled with the query restricted
to names and row counts, so no duration, rate or outcome was available. Rules
were written, committed with a recorded prediction, and only then evaluated. One
San Francisco rule file was amended 27 seconds after being committed, to match on
word boundaries rather than substrings, before that city's data existed on disk.

\textbf{Two authors.} The largest threat to a result like this is that it describes
one person's reasoning. Austin and San Francisco were each given two authors
under the same blind condition: the first was the experimenter, the second a
language model that saw only the taxonomy and was instructed not to look
anything up. New York is the contrast case, with rules written against its own
conditional rates, and Chicago is where those rules are carried.

\section*{Results}

\subsection*{What a blind prior recovers}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{llrrrrr}
\toprule
city & author & AUC & by ticket & by category & ceiling & recovered \\
\midrule
Austin & human & 0.494 & [0.481, 0.508] & [0.331, 0.649] & 0.883 & $-1\%$ \\
Austin & independent & 0.611 & [0.598, 0.626] & [0.442, 0.770] & 0.883 & 29\% \\
San Francisco & human & 0.676 & [0.670, 0.684] & [0.515, 0.815] & 0.886 & 46\% \\
San Francisco & independent & 0.777 & [0.770, 0.784] & [0.529, 0.859] & 0.886 & \textbf{72\%} \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{what a blind prior recovers, by city and by author. Intervals by ticket are 1,000 resamples; by category, 2,000.}
\end{table*}

\textbf{H cannot be answered yes or no} (Table 2). The strongest blind prior recovers
72\% of the achievable skill in San Francisco. The weakest recovers nothing in
Austin. Testing one city would have produced a confident conclusion in either
direction, and the direction would have been an artefact of the city.

The two intervals disagree about what has been shown. By ticket, three of the
four arms sit above chance. By category, only the two San Francisco arms do:
Austin's independent prior spans [0.442, 0.770] and is not distinguishable from
guessing. The headline 72\% spans roughly 7\% to 93\% on the same unit. Every
positive claim below is meant at that width.

\subsection*{What the rules add over structure alone}

A rule set that scores well may be reasoning, or may only be partitioning a
concentrated taxonomy in any sensible way. Two controls separate those.

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
city & arm & AUC & [95\% CI] & recovered \\
\midrule
Austin & blind rules & 0.611 &  & 29\% \\
Austin & category frequency alone & 0.516 & [0.351, 0.726] & 4\% \\
Austin & same rules, verdicts shuffled & 0.501 & [0.322, 0.674] & 0\% \\
San Francisco & blind rules & 0.777 &  & 72\% \\
San Francisco & category frequency alone & 0.672 & [0.441, 0.785] & 45\% \\
San Francisco & same rules, verdicts shuffled & 0.499 & [0.312, 0.690] & 0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{the blind prior against two baselines built from structure alone. Intervals by category.}
\end{table*}

Shuffling the verdicts across categories holds the taxonomy, the row counts and
the exact multiset of scores, and destroys only which category received which
verdict. It scores 0.501 and 0.499 (Table 3), so the reasoning is doing the work
and not the shape of the problem. \textbf{A blind prior beats any structured guess over the same taxonomy.} That is the control this design most needed, and it comes out
in favour of the prior.

Category frequency alone is the more uncomfortable baseline. The blind pull
supplied row counts, and row counts are real operational data. Scored
rarer-is-slower it reaches 0.672 in San Francisco against the human author's
0.676, so \textbf{that arm adds almost nothing beyond volume}. The independent author
clears it by 0.105. Choosing the direction is one bit the catalogue did not
supply, so the baseline is if anything generous.

\subsection*{The author effect is not established}

The independent author beats the experimenter by 0.116 in Austin and 0.101 in
San Francisco, paired on the same resample. The same direction and roughly the
same magnitude twice is suggestive. By ticket both differences exclude zero,
[0.104, 0.131] and [0.095, 0.107]. By category neither does, [-0.036, 0.288] and
[-0.021, 0.174], so two cities do not establish it. An earlier draft reported
these differences with no interval at all.

\subsection*{One department's work explains most of the gap}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering

\caption{excluding everything one Austin department owns, for both authors.}
\end{table*}

Austin Resource Recovery (Table 4) is 34\% of Austin's volume and 70\% of its work runs slow.
Both priors call it fast, mean prediction 0.37. Remove it and the independent
author reaches 70\% against San Francisco's 72\%, the experimenter 38\% against 46\%.
The city gap narrows sharply for both authors.

The reason is worth stating plainly. A missed collection stays open until the
next scheduled route, which is a week away. The work is trivially routine. The
ticket is not, and nothing in the phrase \texttt{ARR - Compost} reveals it.

Two caveats. ARR is a department prefix, not a category called waste, so the
exclusion also removes fast work it owns: \texttt{ARR - Dead Animal Collection} runs
0.0\% slow and \texttt{ARR - General} 8.9\%, both predicted fast and both correct. And
San Francisco has the same failure at a quarter of the scale, which this subset
does not remove: \texttt{Graffiti Public} is 8.2\% of volume, 85.1\% slow, priced 0.37.

\subsection*{The finding}

A blind domain prior recovers roughly 70\% of the achievable skill on work whose
duration follows from the nature of the job, and nothing on work whose duration
is set by an administrative cycle the job description does not reveal. What
determines its value is how much of a client's volume sits in the second group,
and that is not knowable from the taxonomy. The 70\% is the independent author's
in both cities; the experimenter reaches 38\% and 46\% on the same comparison. The
pattern holds for both, the level does not.

\midrule

Rules written with a city's conditional rates in hand reach 81\% there, above the
72\% a blind author reached in San Francisco but not by much.

Carried to Chicago at Chicago's own median they score 0.496, which an earlier
draft called actively misleading. That reading does not survive the thresholds
(Table 5): the medians differ 22-fold, 5.4h against 118.0h, so the carried row
asks a question the rules were never written for. Held at New York's own
threshold they reach 0.581, or 37\%. What remains is mechanical, since the rules
fire on only 17.1\% of Chicago rows, being New York acronyms.

\subsection*{Distribution fidelity does not detect any of this}

\begin{table*}[t]
\centering
\footnotesize
\begin{tabular}{llrrr}
\toprule
task & generator & AUC & recovered & impossible \\
\midrule
city median & real records & 0.918 & 100\% & 0.0\% \\
city median & marginal only & 0.511 & 3\% & 70.5\% \\
city median & marginals + pairwise & 0.917 & 100\% & 0.0\% \\
city median & empirical joint & 0.918 & 100\% & 0.0\% \\
within category & real records & 0.524 & 100\% & 0.0\% \\
within category & marginal only & 0.502 & 10\% & 65.7\% \\
within category & marginals + pairwise & 0.506 & \textbf{25\%} & 0.0\% \\
within category & empirical joint & 0.524 & 100\% & 0.0\% \\
\bottomrule
\end{tabular}
\caption{training on generated New York records, testing on real ones. Ten seeds.}
\end{table*}

Each generator is fitted on real New York records, sampled from, and used to
train a model tested on real ones: train on synthetic, test on real [2]
(Table 6).
Matching marginals and pairwise dependence is the bar PrivBayes and the NIST
differential privacy winner MST fit directly [3, 4], and roughly what SDV's
quality report scores after the fact [5, 6]. That report is a diagnostic
computed on any generator's output, not an objective any of them minimises, and
its own documentation notes that similarity and usefulness may not correlate
[6]. A second recorded prediction, made in advance, was that generated records
at that fidelity would contain impossible combinations and that downstream
accuracy would fall as their rate rose. It failed, and the reason is structural
rather than empirical.

The pairwise generator draws department conditional on category, so it cannot
emit a pair it never saw: 0.0\% impossible is an identity, not a measurement. The
learner is additive in one-hot columns and carries no interaction terms, so a
wrong combination could not have harmed it either. That outcome was fixed by the
design before any data was touched.

The city-median task cannot separate the orders for a further reason: its label
is nearly a function of one column, which a pairwise generator preserves for
free. Labelling each ticket against its own category's median removes that
column, and the orders then separate, pairwise keeping 25\% where the joint keeps
100\%. The ceiling there is 0.524, so the task holds little signal and the ratio
sits on a small base. It is the only measurement here that distinguishes
low-order fidelity from the full joint, and a distribution-level check would
score both generators as near-perfect.

\section*{Limits}

Four cities, one domain, one task family. Municipal services may be unusually
institution-bound, and 311 feeds have three usable columns, no cross-document
structure and no pricing, so they are a thin proxy for the multi-entity
enterprise data the question is really about.

Two blind cities, not four. New York is a contamination control and Chicago its
transfer target, so neither replicates anything. The unit that replicates is the
author effect, and its category intervals cross zero.

The independent author is a language model, and this is the largest uncontrolled
channel in the design. These feeds are among the most widely mirrored public
datasets there are, so blind means the session showed it no durations, not that
its weights hold none. Models are known to have memorised popular tabular
datasets verbatim and to score better on ones they have seen [12], which is the
failure this arm is exposed to, and it produces every headline number. No prompt
or model version was recorded, so it cannot be rerun.

The San Francisco human prior was written knowing what Austin had shown. Its
rules are blind to that city's rates but its author is not blind to the lesson.

The ARR result is a subset chosen after seeing which family the prior got wrong,
in one city: an explanation consistent with the numbers rather than a tested
mechanism. New York carries known artefacts, including a department that closes
most tickets at exactly midnight, so its durations are partly administrative.

That distributional fidelity and downstream utility can diverge is established.
The two rank synthesisers differently [7, 8], high fidelity coexists with poor
classification performance [9], and models trained naively on synthetic data do
not generalise back to real data [10]. Whether the standard metrics measure the
right thing is itself contested [11]. The ladder is here not to re-establish
that, but because it shows a distribution-level check cannot see the effect this
paper measures.

\section*{Reproducing this}

\begin{quote}\small\begin{verbatim}
python run_all.py
\end{verbatim}\end{quote}
Clone the repository linked at the top of this paper and run that. Four public
311 feeds, no key, no account, no cost, numpy and scikit-learn the only
dependencies. The first run downloads about 60MB. It prints every table above,
in order.

Two runs on the same data are byte-identical, since every random draw is seeded.
\texttt{scripts/check\_paper.py} walks every table cell in the markdown source this
document is generated from and fails if a number appears that the run did not
print. It exists because two figures once reached a draft without being computed.
The feeds are live and backfill in place, so it matches within a stated tolerance
and reports how much drift it absorbed. \texttt{data/MANIFEST.json} records the row
count and hash of each file these figures came from, and the run reports any
disagreement before printing.

The blind priors are one file per author per city in \texttt{src/}, each carrying its
reasoning and its recorded prediction.

\section*{What it would take to answer the general question}

Make the prior generate. The arms here compare rule sets against a fitted
conditional on real data, which measures how much of a conditional a domain
expert can guess. The question asked is what generated data costs. Sampling rows
from the taxonomy and volume counts, labelling them with the prior, training on
those and testing on real records would turn a share of achievable margin into
the quantity a buyer needs: how much of the training value of real records a
generator built without them delivers.

Predict the failure families before looking. The ARR explanation implies that any
category whose clock is an administrative cycle rather than the work itself will
break a blind prior. Name those categories in a fifth city from the taxonomy,
record the list, then measure.

More authors, including practitioners, and a language model whose prompt and
version are recorded, would separate the procedure from the person.

The procedural point stands regardless. A prior can only be evaluated as a prior
once per dataset, before anyone has seen the outcomes, and the recorded
prediction has to come first. Of the predictions here, the only one made in
genuine ignorance was the one that turned out wrong.
